Send large files free, without email

Gmail and Outlook cap attachments at 25MB. A short screen recording or a folder of photos blows past that in seconds, and the error message never explains what to do next. Here's what does work.

Last checked 19 August 2026

Why email rejects the file in the first place

Email attachments are base64-encoded, which inflates their size by roughly a third, and mail servers were never designed to move gigabytes around. The 25MB figure isn't arbitrary meanness, it's close to the practical ceiling for a protocol built in the 1980s. Nothing you do to the file will talk your way past it, short of shrinking it.

Send a link instead

  1. Go to dropitover.com and drop the file into "Send a link."
  2. Pick how long the link should work: 1 hour up to 7 days. 24 hours is the default.
  3. You get a link and a QR code. Send the link however you'd normally send anything: text, email, chat, whatever the recipient actually checks.
  4. They open it and download directly. No account, on either end.

The file is encrypted before it's stored, and it's gone automatically once the link expires or, if you turn that option on, the moment it's been downloaded once.

If the other person is right next to you

Skip the link entirely. Open the site on both devices on the same Wi-Fi and they'll find each other automatically, sending the file straight from one browser to the other with no upload step at all.

What still won't fit

2 GB covers nearly everything: video clips, large photo batches, zipped project folders, uncompressed audio. A feature-length uncompressed video or a full disk image can exceed it. For those, splitting the file with a zip tool into smaller parts, or sending as a room transfer where size is limited only by the sending device's memory, are the practical options.

Is it actually safe to use a link like this?

The file is encrypted before it's written to storage, using a key that's different for every file. Nobody can guess a link either: it's a long random string, not a sequential id, so there's nothing to enumerate. The two things worth doing on your side are picking the shortest expiry that still gives the recipient enough time, and turning on "delete after download" for anything you'd rather not sit around even encrypted.

What happens after the link expires

The file is deleted automatically, not just hidden. A background job checks for expired shares every few minutes and removes them, and opening an expired link returns nothing to recover, on either end. There's no trace left to clean up afterward.
